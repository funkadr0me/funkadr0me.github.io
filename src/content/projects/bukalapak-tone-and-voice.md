## Two voices, one brand

In December 2017, I opened the Bukalapak app for a job test. At the top, a banner said "Nego Terus Sampai Jodoh" (Keep haggling till you find the one), as cheeky as its TV ads. Right under it sat a menu in all caps. Two voices, one screen.

I joined the next year. By 2019, the app had warmed up, but it still sounded like several people taking turns (we had nine writers, and you could tell who wrote what). Big tech brands were learning to speak as one, and our design team had started talking to brand and marketing. Nobody had decided how Bukalapak should sound, so we set out to find out.

## Listening first

In March 2019, we interviewed ten stakeholders from design, research, product and marketing. We asked what users needed from our words, what kind of character they'd look up to, and how we sounded next to other apps.

Most agreed we'd come a long way ("It used to be so robotic," one said). What they wanted next was one voice. One designer put it simply: however many writers we had, people should hear one person talking.

Some still wanted a "proper" voice for serious things like payments, because that's how trust is built. But one story from the interviews stuck with me. When a seller's package broke on the way, our app blamed the seller at once. A competitor helped them find out what happened first. Reading between the lines, stern words weren't building trust (least of all outside the big cities). All users wanted was a little sincere help.

## One day, two kinds of writers

By then, both writing teams reported to me: the product content strategists, who wrote the app, and the creative content strategists, who wrote the ads. On 21 March, two teammates and I brought all nine writers into one room. Ad writers have a few seconds to sell, so their headlines are in your face. Product writers walk people through a task. We needed both.

First, we listed what confused our users: long copy, jargon, lines that said nothing. Then we played a word game with one rule: Be X, not Y. That's where "Be a good friend, not a salesman" came from.

The liveliest round was "Who says it?" One side pictured a sharp consultant (the kind with a slide for everything). The other pictured a barber. The barber won. You trust a barber: they remember your name, hold sharp tools to your head, and give advice when you ask.

The barber stayed in our guide for a while. Then our UX researchers advised against leaning too hard on personas, and management asked us to let him go. The principles carried on without him.

## Be a good friend, not a salesman

Each principle started as "Be X, not Y". For the brand guideline, they later got shorter names:

- User-centric. Tell people only what matters most, and make them the subject of the action.
- Be a good friend. Every line is a thoughtful suggestion that helps them decide quickly.
- Offer solutions. Every word guides people toward their goal.
- Use plain words. Common words with clear meanings, and no new jargon unless we're teaching it.
- Be consistent. Everything comes from one big idea.

Be a good friend did the most work (the barber lives on in it). The old app told people what went wrong and stopped there. I put it bluntly in our guide: user experience should not be a thriller, so every line had to help.

An example from our dialog guideline: "BukaDompet Kamu Dibekukan Sementara. Kamu dapat menggunakan metode bayar lain." (Your BukaDompet is temporarily frozen. You can use another payment method.)

We also set the tone by how the user feels:

- Joyful when it goes well: "Hore! Pesananmu sudah sampai" (Hooray! Your order has arrived)
- Casual when it's neutral: "Barang telah sampai di kota pengantaran" (The item has reached the delivery city)
- Serious when it goes wrong: "Maaf, pembayaran tidak berhasil. Mohon ulangi beberapa waktu lagi." (Sorry, the payment didn't go through. Please try again in a while.)

We said sorry when the fault was likely ours, or when the stakes were high, like a payment. We didn't when things were neutral or the fix was obvious, like an item out of stock. Sorry isn't a debt we pay in every message. We say it when it's due.

## Words that come with the component

The interviews also showed that designers often wrote their own error messages rather than wait for a writer. So I brought the principles into Bazaar, Bukalapak's design system, at the component level. When designers pick up a component, its copy framework comes along, so the voice travels even when no writer is in the room.

## There's always a way

While we wrote, the creative team was building a new brand campaign: "Ada Aja Caranya" (There's always a way). We met every two weeks, and they used our principles to guide the campaign's voice. Management backed the voice too, in the product's design and with a bigger role in the in-app campaigns that followed.

Today, the tone and voice sits in Bukalapak's brand guideline, on the same page as the logo and the colours. The two voices that shared one screen in 2017 now share one page.
